Tile Layout Mistakes That Cost You Extra Boxes

← All Guides

Run a floor through the tile calculator and it does clean, straightforward math — area divided by tile size, plus a waste allowance. That math is correct, but it assumes a simple grid layout. The way you actually lay the tile can push real waste well past the number on the page.

Diagonal and Herringbone Patterns Waste More

A straight grid layout wastes tile mostly at the room's edges, where whole tiles don't fit and need a single cut. Diagonal layouts and herringbone patterns create angled cuts on every edge tile, and those angled offcuts usually can't be reused elsewhere the way a straight cut sometimes can. If you're doing anything other than a simple grid, bump the waste allowance from the standard 10% up to 15-20%.

Grout Line Width Adds Up

A wider grout line (3/8" vs 1/16", for example) takes up real space across a whole floor — enough that it slightly reduces the number of full tiles that fit in a given area. It's a small effect per tile, but on a large floor with wide grout lines, it's worth accounting for rather than assuming tile dimensions alone tell the whole story.

Rule of thumb: the fancier the layout, the higher the waste allowance. Simple grid: 10%. Diagonal or herringbone: 15-20%.

Buy Extra for Future Repairs

Tile is sold in dye lots, and lots vary slightly in color and shade — sometimes enough to notice. If a tile cracks two years from now and you buy a "replacement" of the same product line, there's a real chance it won't match perfectly since your original lot is long gone from the shelf. Buying one or two extra boxes beyond your calculated waste, and storing them, is cheap insurance against a mismatched patch job down the road.

Start From the Focal Point, Not the Wall

A common beginner mistake is starting the layout flush against one wall and working across the room. Whatever ugly partial-tile cut is left over lands wherever the math runs out — sometimes right in the most visible spot in the room. Professionals typically start from the room's focal point (the entryway, the center of a kitchen island, the most visible sightline) and work outward, so any necessary partial-tile cuts end up hidden near less visible walls or under cabinetry instead of front and center.
