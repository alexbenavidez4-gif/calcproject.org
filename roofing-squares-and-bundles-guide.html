Roofing Squares and Bundles: How to Estimate Shingles Without Guessing

← All Guides

Roofing materials are sold in units that confuse almost everyone the first time. Shingles come in bundles, bundles add up to squares, and the size of your roof is not the same as the size of your house. This guide explains each term, shows how roof pitch changes your numbers, and walks through an example you can copy.

What Is a Roofing Square?

A roofing square is 100 square feet of roof. A roof that is 1,500 square feet is 15 squares. Contractors quote prices per square, and shingle packaging is labeled to match. Nearly all standard shingles are sold in bundles, and it usually takes about three bundles to cover one square. Some heavier architectural or specialty shingles take four bundles per square, so always check the label on the product you buy.

Your Roof Is Bigger Than Your House's Footprint

A common beginner mistake is to measure the length and width of the house and call that the roof area. A sloped roof has more surface than the floor beneath it. The steeper the roof, the greater the difference. Roof steepness is called pitch, and it is written as a ratio such as 6/12, meaning the roof rises 6 inches for every 12 inches of horizontal run.

To convert the footprint to the actual roof area, multiply by a pitch factor:

  • 4/12 pitch: multiply by about 1.05
  • 6/12 pitch: multiply by about 1.12
  • 8/12 pitch: multiply by about 1.20
  • 10/12 pitch: multiply by about 1.30
  • 12/12 pitch: multiply by about 1.41

You can find your pitch by placing a level on the underside of a rafter in the attic, measuring 12 inches along the level, and then measuring the vertical distance from the end of the level up to the rafter. Do not climb onto a roof to measure it. Roofs are slippery, and falls are one of the most serious DIY dangers.

Worked Example

Say a house is 40 feet by 30 feet, with a simple gable roof at 6/12 pitch.

  1. Footprint: 40 x 30 = 1,200 square feet.
  2. Apply the pitch factor: 1,200 x 1.118 = about 1,342 square feet of roof.
  3. Think about overhangs. Eaves that extend past the walls add roof area, and a 1-foot overhang on all sides adds roughly 12 percent for a house this size. This example ignores them, so if your overhangs are large, measure the footprint from the outer edge of the eaves instead.
  4. Add 10 percent for waste, because of cuts at the ridge, valleys, and edges: about 1,476 square feet.
  5. Convert to squares: 1,476 / 100 = 14.8, so round up to 15 squares.
  6. Bundles: 15 x 3 = 45 bundles.

How Much Waste to Allow

A simple gable roof can get by with about 10 percent. A roof with many valleys, dormers, hips, or chimneys needs 15 percent or more, because every angle produces offcuts that cannot be reused. A complex roof is also a good reason to ask a professional for a quote.

What Else Goes on a Roof

Shingles are only one item on the list. A roofing job may also need:

  • Underlayment: a water-resistant layer under the shingles.
  • Drip edge: metal strips along the eaves and rakes that guide water into the gutters.
  • Starter strips: the first row at the eave, which seals the bottom edge.
  • Ridge cap shingles: special pieces that cover the peak. These are sold by the linear foot of ridge.
  • Flashing: metal around chimneys, vents, and valleys, which is where most leaks start.
  • Roofing nails of the right length.

Tear-Off vs. Layover

Some local codes allow a second layer of shingles over the first, but removing the old layer lets you inspect the wood underneath, and most roofers recommend it. Tear-off adds disposal costs and labor, so factor it into your budget.

Safety first: roofing is dangerous work. For anything steeper than a gentle slope, or higher than one story, hiring a professional is often the safest and, once you count the tools and risk, not much more expensive choice.
