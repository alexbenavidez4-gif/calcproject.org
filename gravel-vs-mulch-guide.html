Gravel vs. Mulch: Which Is Right for Your Yard?

← All Guides

Both show up in the same aisle at the garden center, both get spread over dirt, and both get lumped together in casual conversation as "yard stuff." They actually solve pretty different problems, and picking the wrong one means redoing the work in a year or two.

What mulch is actually for

Mulch belongs around plants — garden beds, tree rings, flower borders. Its job is to keep soil moisture in, block weed growth while things are actively growing, and slowly break down into the soil, adding organic matter as it goes. That decomposition is a feature, not a flaw, but it means mulch needs to be topped up every year, usually in spring. It's the cheaper material upfront, but it's a recurring cost, not a one-time purchase.

What gravel is actually for

Gravel belongs where you want permanence and drainage — walkways, driveways, drainage beds around a home's foundation, or areas where nothing is meant to grow. It doesn't break down, doesn't need yearly refreshing, and handles foot or vehicle traffic that would compact and ruin a mulch bed. The tradeoff is cost and effort up front: gravel needs a landscape fabric barrier underneath to keep weeds from pushing through, and once it's down, moving or removing it later is real work.

The simple test: if something is supposed to grow there, use mulch. If nothing is supposed to grow there, use gravel.

Cost over time, not just at checkout

Mulch is usually cheaper per bag or per yard, but because it needs replacing annually, the cost repeats. Gravel costs more initially and needs that fabric barrier as an added expense, but a well-installed gravel bed can last a decade or more with just occasional raking or weeding. For a small garden bed, mulch's lower upfront cost wins easily. For a driveway or a path you'll use for years, gravel's one-time cost usually comes out ahead.

Using both together

Plenty of yards use both, and that's often the right call — a gravel path running through a yard, bordered by mulched garden beds on either side. The gravel handles foot traffic and drainage where you're walking; the mulch handles moisture retention and weed suppression where things are actually planted. Just make sure there's a clear edging (plastic, metal, or stone) between the two, or the gravel and mulch will slowly migrate into each other over a season or two of rain and foot traffic.

Before you calculate either one

Measure the actual area you're covering, not the whole yard — it's common to overestimate a bed's size by eye. For gravel, decide on depth based on use: 2 inches for a light decorative bed, 3-4 inches for a walking path, 4-6 inches for anything vehicles will drive on. For mulch, 2-3 inches is the standard depth around most plants; piling it deeper than that can actually suffocate roots rather than help them.
